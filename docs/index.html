Offload grayscale, blur, gradients, and edge extract to a Gowin FPGA on the Brisbane EPM11, solve tag pose on the RP2350, and publish NetworkTables 4 telemetry to a Raspberry Pi 5 running Systemcore Alpha. Hands-on lab work — not a dark dashboard.

Why we're building this

USB webcams burn ~15 ms in compression and Linux buffer stalls before your detector even starts. A 24-pin DVP parallel stream into the GW1NR-9 keeps pixels in hardware — sub-0.05 ms front-end latency — so the dual Cortex-M33 on the RP2350 can spend cycles on 3D homography instead of waiting on USB. Ethan Tola’s RIT thesis (Fusion 2 / Zynq 7020) showed ~43 fps FPGA vs ~2 fps pure software; we’re chasing that win on a cheaper Brisbane + OV2640 BOM for multi-camera odometry.

OV2640 DVP — 24-pin parallel raw pixel stream

OV2640 streams raw pixels over a 24-pin Digital Video Port at 60 FPS with a 24.0 MHz PCLK. Zero Linux buffer stalls. On EPM11 there is no native camera connector — use a 0.5 mm FFC-to-DIP breakout and solder to headers. First milestone after soldering: verify live pixels into the FPGA.

Student callout — hardware latency vs USB webcam DVP → FPGA front-end latency is sub-0.05 ms (pixels stay on the wire / fabric). A typical USB webcam path spends ~15 ms in MJPEG/YUY2 compression + kernel buffering before your detector sees a frame. That gap is why we solder the FFC breakout instead of plugging a UVC cam.
⚠
No Dupont jumpers on the 24-pin 0.5 mm DVP FFC

Parasitic capacitance on Dupont wires will corrupt the 24.0 MHz PCLK edge. Solder the breakout to headers. Period.

⚠
CAN terminators MUST BE 120 Ω — NOT 120 kΩ

Place a 120 ohm resistor across CAN-H and CAN-L at both ends of the bus (unpowered). With both terminators in parallel you should read ~60.0 Ω loop resistance. If you read 120 Ω, one terminator is missing. 120 kΩ is a common parts-bin mistake and will kill the bus.

⚠
Feather = classic CAN ≤ 1 Mbps — NOT CAN-FD

The Feather RP2040 CAN (MCP25625) path is classic CAN only. Do not assume FD frames or 5 Mbps payload rates on that adapter.

📝 Bench sticky Verify 60 Ω loop before applying 12 V to any CAN device. Write the meter reading in the lab notebook.

⚠
Pi 5 = software stand-in only — no 24 V protection / industrial clamps

Pi 5 GPIO pins lack 24 V industrial protection and 50 V inductive kick clamp diodes. Treat this as a software / NT / deploy stand-in — not an electrical twin of Systemcore.

NEW AUTOMATION TEST CHASSIS

FRC Prototype Test Robot

Simple mecanum prototype for team automation testing — not a competition robot. Goal: drive 4–5 brushed DC motors with SystemCore SmartIO, practice automation scripts without tying up the full bot.

Motor control

Recommended: 2× TB6612FNG dual H-bridge modules (Toshiba).

  • ~1.2 A continuous / 3.2 A peak per channel
  • Motor supply 2.5–13.5 V · logic 2.7–5.5 V (3.3 V SmartIO OK)
  • Standby, thermal shutdown, kickback diodes
  • Two modules → four mecanum drive motors

Alternative: Adafruit Motor Shield V2 (four TB6612FNG chips, I2C + Adafruit_MotorShield library) when you need to free SmartIO PWM/DIR pins.

Inventory highlights

  • 2× TB6612FNG or 1× Motor Shield V2
  • 4–5 brushed DC motors + mecanum wheel kit
  • SystemCore (or Alpha stand-in) + SmartIO access
  • Separate motor PSU, common ground, breadboard/perfboard
  • Fuse / kill switch recommended

Build steps (summary)

  1. Assemble mecanum chassis and mount motors/wheels.
  2. Mount SystemCore + drivers; wire separate VM motor supply and common GND.
  3. Wire SmartIO PWM/DIR/STBY (TB6612 path) or I2C (Motor Shield V2).
  4. Label FL/FR/BL/BR; bring up one motor at current-limited low voltage.
  5. Implement mecanum mix (vx, vy, omega → four wheel speeds) and enable/deadman before automation scripts.
Power note: Do not power motor VM from logic rails. Tie grounds together; fuse VM+.
